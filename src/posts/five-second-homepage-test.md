Most visitors make up their minds about a website almost immediately. They glance at the first screen, decide whether they are in the right place and either keep reading or leave. That first glance is the most valuable real estate your company owns online, and it is very often wasted.

The five-second test is a simple way to check whether your homepage survives that first glance. It costs nothing, takes a few minutes and almost always reveals something worth fixing.

## How to run the test

Find five people who do not know your company well - ideally people similar to your buyers. Show them your homepage for five seconds, then hide it and ask three questions:

1. **What does this company do?**
2. **Who is it for?**
3. **What would you do next if you were interested?**

Write down their answers word for word. If most people cannot answer all three clearly, the homepage is costing you business.

## Why most homepages fail

The reasons are remarkably consistent across industries:

- **Vague headlines.** Phrases like "Empowering the future" or "Excellence in every detail" sound pleasant and say nothing.
- **Clever over clear.** Wordplay and metaphors that make sense to the team that wrote them but confuse everyone else.
- **Too many messages.** Sliders, multiple headlines and competing calls to action that dilute attention.
- **Hidden next steps.** The contact button is small, vague or buried below the fold.
- **Decorative images.** Stock photos that add mood but no meaning.

> If a visitor has to scroll to understand what you do, most of them never will.

## What a strong first screen contains

A homepage that passes the test usually has five elements, all visible without scrolling:

- **A headline that states what you do and for whom** - in plain language.
- **A supporting line that explains the outcome** - what changes for the customer.
- **One primary action** - such as "Get a quote" or "Book a call" - and at most one secondary action.
- **A signal of credibility** - years in business, the type of clients you serve or a clear process.
- **A visual that supports the message** - showing the product, the work or the outcome rather than generic decoration.

## Writing a better headline

The headline does most of the work. A useful formula is: what you do, for whom, with what result. For example:

- "Fast, search-ready websites for industrial companies that want more qualified enquiries."
- "Brand and sales material for technical products that are hard to explain."

These are not poetic, but they are clear. Once clarity is achieved, you can refine the tone - but never at the expense of understanding.

## Beyond the first screen

Passing the five-second test earns the visitor's attention; the rest of the page must keep it. A good homepage then answers the questions a buyer naturally asks, in order:

1. Do you understand my problem?
2. How do you solve it?
3. Can I trust you?
4. What does working with you look like?
5. What do I do next?

Each section should answer one of these questions, and every section should end with a clear way to take the next step.

## Measure after you change

Once you have improved the first screen, watch what happens. Useful signals include how far people scroll, how many click your primary action and how many enquiries arrive from the homepage. Small changes to a headline or call to action can have a surprising effect on these numbers.

Run the five-second test again with new people after each significant change. It is quick enough to become a habit.

## The bottom line

Your homepage is judged in seconds by people who owe you nothing. Make those seconds count: say clearly what you do, who it is for and what to do next. Clarity is not the enemy of good design - it is what makes good design worth looking at.
