Few marketing decisions last as long as a name. Logos are refreshed, websites rebuilt and campaigns replaced, but a product or company name may stay in use for decades. It will be said in meetings, typed into search engines and written in contracts thousands of times.

Yet names are often chosen quickly, by committee, under deadline pressure. The result is too many names that are forgettable, hard to pronounce or impossible to search for.

## What a good name does

A strong name:

- **Is easy to say and spell**, so people can recommend it without hesitation.
- **Is distinctive** within its market, so it is not confused with competitors.
- **Is memorable**, often through rhythm, simplicity or a clear idea.
- **Leaves room to grow**, without tying the company to something too narrow.
- **Works in the markets you serve**, without unfortunate meanings elsewhere.

> The best names are easy to say, hard to forget and impossible to confuse.

## The main types of names

Names generally fall into a few families:

- **Descriptive** - say what the thing does. Clear, but often hard to protect and easy to forget.
- **Suggestive** - hint at a benefit or quality. A good balance of meaning and distinctiveness.
- **Abstract** - invented or borrowed words with no direct meaning. Highly distinctive, but require more effort to build recognition.
- **Founder or place names** - personal and traditional, but can limit growth or create confusion.

There is no single correct type. The right choice depends on your market, ambitions and budget for building awareness.

## A practical naming process

### 1. Write a naming brief

Describe what is being named, who it is for, what it should communicate and any constraints. Include names you admire and names you want to avoid.

### 2. Generate widely

Create a long list - often hundreds of options - before judging any of them. Early criticism kills good ideas.

### 3. Filter with criteria

Reduce the list using clear tests: pronunciation, spelling, distinctiveness, meaning, availability of domains and social handles, and initial trademark checks.

### 4. Test with real people

Say shortlisted names out loud to people outside the project. Ask them to spell the name after hearing it, and to describe what it suggests. Their reactions reveal practical problems quickly.

### 5. Check legal and linguistic risks

Before deciding, confirm trademark availability with a professional and check meanings in the languages of your key markets.

## Avoid common traps

- **Choosing by committee preference.** Decide using criteria, not majority vote.
- **Over-explaining.** Names do not need to describe everything; that is the job of the tagline and the brand.
- **Following trends.** Fashionable spellings and suffixes date quickly.
- **Ignoring how it sounds.** A name that looks good written but is awkward to say will struggle.

## Give the name time

Most new names feel strange at first, even good ones. Familiarity grows with use. Resist the urge to change a carefully chosen name simply because it feels unfamiliar in the first weeks.

## The bottom line

A name is one of the longest-lasting assets a company owns. Choose it with a clear brief, broad exploration, practical tests and professional checks. Done well, it will work quietly for you every time someone says it.
