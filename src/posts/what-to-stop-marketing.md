At the start of each year, marketing plans usually grow. New channels are added, new campaigns proposed and new tools purchased. What is rarely discussed is what should stop. As a result, budgets and teams are spread across too many activities, and none of them receives enough attention to succeed.

The most valuable marketing decision a leadership team can make is often not what to start, but what to stop.

## Why activities accumulate

Marketing activities are easy to start and awkward to end. Someone championed each one, a contract may be in place and stopping can feel like admitting failure. Over time, the plan becomes a collection of habits rather than a strategy.

Signs of accumulation include:

- Channels maintained because "we have always done it."
- Campaigns with no clear goal or measurement.
- Tools that few people use.
- Content produced to a schedule rather than for an audience.

> Focus is not doing more of everything. It is doing more of what works.

## A simple stop-start-continue review

Once a year, list every marketing activity and ask three questions of each:

1. **What is it for?** Name the specific goal - for example, qualified enquiries from a target segment.
2. **What evidence do we have?** Look at results, even if they are imperfect.
3. **What would happen if we stopped?** Be honest about the real impact.

Then sort each activity into one of three groups:

- **Stop** - no clear goal, no evidence of impact or easily replaced.
- **Continue** - clear goal and reasonable evidence of contribution.
- **Increase** - strong results that could grow with more investment.

## Give compounding activities time

Some activities show results quickly; others compound slowly. Paid advertising can produce leads within weeks, while brand building, search visibility and thought leadership take months to mature. Judging them on the same timeline leads to stopping valuable work too early.

Agree in advance how long each activity will be given before it is judged.

## Concentrate the savings

Money saved by stopping weak activities should not simply disappear from the budget. Reinvesting it in proven activities is how focus turns into growth. A smaller number of well-funded efforts almost always outperforms a long list of underfunded ones.

## Watch for false economies

Some cuts look sensible but cost more later. Examples include:

- Letting the website fall out of date.
- Stopping all content production, losing momentum built over years.
- Abandoning past clients and contacts who could become future business.

These foundations support everything else. Cut carefully.

## Make the review a habit

The stop-start-continue review works best as a regular rhythm - annually for the full plan, quarterly for a lighter check. Over time, it creates a culture where marketing activities must earn their place.

## The bottom line

Every activity you continue is a choice not to invest that money and attention elsewhere. Review honestly, stop what does not work, give compounding efforts time and concentrate resources where they deliver. The result is a smaller, sharper marketing plan that achieves more.
