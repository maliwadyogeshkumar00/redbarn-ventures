Launch day is exciting. The new logo is revealed, the website goes live and the announcement goes out. Then, quietly, things start to slip. Old templates reappear. Sales teams keep using last year's deck. Social posts mix the old and new styles. Within a few months, the company looks less consistent than before the rebrand.

Most rebrands do not fail because of the design. They fail because of the rollout.

## The launch-day illusion

Leadership teams often treat a rebrand as a project with an end date. In reality, launch is the start of a change in behaviour across the whole organisation. Hundreds of people create materials every week, and each one needs to adopt the new brand for it to take hold.

> A rebrand succeeds when the newest employee uses it correctly without asking anyone.

## Five reasons rebrands lose momentum

- **Assets are hard to find.** New logos and templates are buried in a shared drive nobody can navigate.
- **Old materials stay in circulation.** Previous decks, brochures and email signatures keep being reused.
- **People were not told why.** Teams see the change as cosmetic and do not understand what it represents.
- **No owner after launch.** The project team disbands, and nobody answers questions.
- **Too much changes at once.** Every touchpoint is updated in a rush, and quality suffers.

## A ninety-day rollout plan

### Before launch: prepare the tools

- Build a simple brand hub with logos, colours, fonts, templates and examples.
- Prepare the most-used templates first: presentation, document, email signature and social posts.
- Brief managers on the reasons behind the change so they can explain it to their teams.

### Days 1 to 30: update the most visible touchpoints

Focus on what customers see most: website, sales deck, email signatures, social profiles and proposals. Retire old versions deliberately - delete or archive them so they cannot be reused by accident.

### Days 31 to 60: train and support

Run short sessions for teams that create materials most often: sales, marketing, support and leadership. Show them where to find assets and how to use templates. Appoint a brand owner who can answer questions quickly.

### Days 61 to 90: audit and fix

Review a sample of real materials produced since launch. Where are people still using old versions? Which templates are missing or hard to use? Fix the gaps and share examples of good use.

## Tell the story inside first

Employees are the most important audience for a rebrand. If they understand what the new brand stands for - and why the company changed - they become its strongest advocates. If they see only a new logo, they will treat it as decoration.

Share the reasoning, the new positioning and what it means for how people work and speak, not just how materials look.

## Phase what does not need to change immediately

Not every item needs updating on day one. Signage, vehicles, packaging and printed stock can often be updated as they are naturally replaced. A clear phasing plan avoids waste and keeps attention on high-impact touchpoints.

## The bottom line

The design gets a rebrand started. The rollout decides whether it survives. Make assets easy to find, retire the old versions, explain the reasons and keep someone responsible for at least ninety days. Do that, and the new brand becomes the company's natural way of showing up.
