Chief executives are rightly told to delegate. Marketing teams and agencies have specialist skills, and a CEO who approves every social post is wasting time. Yet some marketing decisions are so closely tied to strategy, valuation and reputation that delegating them entirely is a mistake.

The difference between companies with strong, coherent brands and those without often comes down to whether the CEO stayed involved in these four decisions.

## 1. Who the company is for

Choosing the target audience is a strategic decision, not a marketing one. It determines which products to build, which markets to enter, which salespeople to hire and which opportunities to decline. Marketing can research and recommend, but the final choice shapes the whole business.

When the CEO is clear about who the company serves best, every department can align around it. When that clarity is missing, marketing ends up guessing - and spreading budget too thin.

## 2. What the company stands for

Positioning - the one idea a company wants to own in buyers' minds - is too important to be decided only in a marketing workshop. It needs to reflect the CEO's view of where the company is going and what it will be known for in five years.

A useful test: can the CEO explain the company's position in one sentence, the same way, in every meeting? If not, the position is not yet decided.

> Positioning is strategy expressed in language. That makes it a leadership decision.

## 3. How much to invest, and for how long

Marketing budgets are often set as a percentage of revenue and trimmed when times are hard. But the most effective marketing - brand building, content, search visibility and long-term relationships - compounds over years. Cutting it abruptly can undo progress that took a long time to build.

The CEO should decide the level and duration of commitment, so marketing teams can plan beyond the next quarter and measure results over a realistic period.

## 4. The standard of quality

Every company has an unspoken standard for how good its materials, website and communication need to be. That standard is usually set by the CEO, whether deliberately or not. If leadership accepts mediocre decks and outdated websites, the organisation will too.

Setting a clear bar - and occasionally sending work back - signals that the company's public face matters.

## What to delegate

Almost everything else should be handed to the marketing team and trusted partners:

- Choosing channels and tactics.
- Planning and producing content.
- Running campaigns and optimising performance.
- Managing tools, data and reporting.
- Day-to-day design and copy decisions within the brand system.

The CEO's role here is to ask for clear results and to remove obstacles, not to review every detail.

## Make involvement efficient

Staying involved does not require hours each week. A practical rhythm:

- **Quarterly**, review the audience, positioning and key results with the marketing lead.
- **Twice a year**, review the website, sales materials and brand consistency personally.
- **Annually**, agree the budget and long-term priorities.

This keeps the strategic decisions where they belong without slowing the team down.

## The bottom line

Great marketing teams need room to execute and clarity to aim at. CEOs who own the audience, the position, the investment horizon and the quality bar give their teams both. Everything else can - and should - be delegated.
