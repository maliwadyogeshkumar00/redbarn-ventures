Ask most chief executives when they last looked closely at their own company website and the honest answer is often: when it launched. Yet the website is usually the first place a potential customer, partner, investor or senior hire forms an opinion of the business. Long before anyone reads your proposal or meets your team, they have already decided whether you look credible, current and capable.

That makes the website a board-level asset, not a marketing side project. This guide explains how leadership teams can judge it with the same rigour they apply to any other important investment - without needing to be designers or developers.

## Who is really visiting your website

It is tempting to think of the website as a tool for attracting new customers. It does that, but it has at least five audiences, each with different questions:

- **Prospective customers** want to know quickly whether you solve their problem, whether you are credible and what the next step is.
- **Existing customers** look for support, documentation, product updates and reassurance that they chose well.
- **Candidates** check whether your company is somewhere ambitious people want to work.
- **Partners and suppliers** assess whether you are a serious, stable business.
- **Investors, lenders and acquirers** use your website as an early signal of how well the company is run.

A website that serves only one of these audiences leaves value on the table. A website that confuses all of them actively costs the business opportunities it never hears about.

> Most lost deals never become a conversation. The buyer simply looked at your website, compared it with a competitor's and moved on.

## The five questions every CEO should ask

You do not need technical knowledge to evaluate a website. Sit down with it on your phone and on a laptop and ask these five questions.

### 1. Is it clear within five seconds what we do and for whom?

Look at the first screen of the home page. Would a stranger understand what your company does, who it serves and why it is different - without scrolling? Vague slogans, industry jargon and generic stock images are the most common reasons the answer is no.

### 2. Does it look like the company we are today?

Companies change faster than their websites. If your product range, positioning, scale or ambition has moved on but the website has not, visitors meet a smaller, older version of your business. That gap quietly undermines trust and pricing power.

### 3. Is the next step obvious on every page?

Every important page should end with a clear action: request a quote, book a call, download a datasheet, contact sales. If a motivated buyer has to search for how to get in touch, some of them will not bother.

### 4. Is it fast, secure and comfortable on a phone?

Slow pages, broken layouts on mobile and security warnings all signal neglect. They also hurt your visibility in search engines. Ask your team for a simple speed and accessibility check - it takes minutes.

### 5. Can our own team update it?

If every new product page, case study or job advertisement requires a developer and several weeks, the website will always be out of date. A modern website should let non-technical colleagues make everyday changes safely.

## Warning signs that the website is costing you business

Some problems are obvious once you know to look for them. Treat any of these as a signal to act:

- The sales team avoids sending prospects to the website, or apologises for it.
- Competitors with weaker products look more established online.
- Enquiries from the website are rare or poorly qualified.
- Important pages contain outdated information, people who have left or products you no longer sell.
- Nobody can say how many enquiries the website produced last quarter.
- The website was built by someone who is no longer available, and nobody fully controls the domain, hosting or code.

That last point deserves special attention. Many companies discover too late that their website, domain or analytics are registered to a former employee or a supplier they no longer work with. Make sure the company owns and controls every account.

## What a strong company website does differently

The best company websites are not necessarily the most elaborate. They share a small number of qualities that leadership teams can recognise and insist on.

### They lead with the customer's problem

Strong websites start with the problem their buyers face and the outcome they want, then explain how the company delivers it. Weak websites start with the company's history and a list of everything it does.

### They prove credibility without exaggeration

Clear explanations, specific descriptions of the work, transparent processes, well-designed product information and honest answers to common questions build more trust than superlatives ever can.

### They are designed as one system with the brand

The website, sales decks, datasheets, social media and trade show materials should look and sound like the same company. When they do, every touchpoint reinforces the others.

### They are built to be found

Search engines and, increasingly, AI assistants are where buyers begin their research. A strong website is structured so that both people and machines can understand who the company is, what it offers and which questions it answers.

### They are measured

Leadership should know how many people visit, which pages they read, how many enquiries the site produces and how many of those turn into revenue. Without these numbers, the website cannot be managed like an asset.

## The rise of AI search

For most of the last two decades, being visible online meant ranking in traditional search results. That is changing. More buyers now ask AI assistants for recommendations, comparisons and explanations, and those assistants draw on the content they can find and trust.

For leadership teams, this has three practical implications:

1. **Clear, factual content matters more than ever.** Pages that plainly explain what you do, for whom and how are more likely to be understood and cited.
2. **Answering real questions is valuable.** Well-written answers to the questions buyers actually ask - about price drivers, timelines, comparisons and risks - help both search engines and AI assistants.
3. **Consistency across the web helps.** Your website, company profiles and published content should describe the business in the same way.

## How to decide between a refresh and a rebuild

Not every website needs to be replaced. A simple test helps leadership teams decide.

**A refresh** is usually enough when the structure works, the platform is modern and easy to edit, and the main problems are outdated content, design or messaging.

**A rebuild** is the better choice when the website is slow or insecure, the platform is hard to maintain, the structure no longer reflects the business, or the brand itself is changing.

Either way, the project should start with clarity on audiences, messages and goals - not with colours and layouts.

> Design is the last decision in a good website project, not the first.

## Five numbers leadership should see every quarter

You do not need a complex dashboard. These five numbers, reviewed every quarter, tell leadership almost everything it needs to know:

1. **Qualified visitors** - people from your target markets and segments, not just total traffic.
2. **Enquiries** - forms, calls and emails that started on the website.
3. **Conversion rate** - the share of qualified visitors who make an enquiry.
4. **Pipeline from the website** - the value of opportunities that began online.
5. **Speed and health** - page speed, uptime and any security issues.

When these numbers move in the right direction, the website is working. When they stall, leadership knows exactly where to look.

## Four myths that hold companies back

Some beliefs about websites persist in boardrooms long after they have stopped being true.

**"Our customers do not buy online, so the website does not matter."** Even in complex, relationship-led sales, buyers research online first. The website decides whether you make the shortlist.

**"A beautiful design will fix it."** Design matters, but clarity, content and structure matter more. A beautiful website that does not answer buyers' questions still loses deals.

**"We will do it properly once the business is bigger."** An out-of-date website makes it harder to become bigger. It is one of the cheapest ways to look as established as you want to be.

**"Once it is built, it is finished."** Websites decline quietly every month they are not maintained. The best ones are improved continuously.

## Governance: who owns the website?

Websites decay when nobody owns them. Leadership teams should make three decisions explicitly:

- **A business owner** responsible for the website's performance, usually in marketing or commercial leadership.
- **A content process** that keeps products, people, news and answers up to date, with clear responsibilities across departments.
- **A review rhythm** - typically quarterly - where leadership looks at traffic, enquiries and conversion, and agrees on improvements.

With these in place, the website stops being a project that happens every five years and becomes an asset that improves every quarter.

## Questions to ask your marketing team

If you want a quick read on the health of your website without a formal review, ask your marketing or commercial lead these questions at your next meeting:

- Which three pages do our best customers read before they contact us?
- How quickly do we reply to website enquiries, and who is responsible?
- When did we last update our product or service pages?
- Who controls our domain, hosting and analytics accounts today?
- What would we change first if we had a modest budget next month?

Clear, confident answers suggest the website is being managed. Uncertain answers are a signal that it needs an owner.

## What to expect from a good website partner

If you work with an external partner, expect more than design and code. A good partner should:

- Start by understanding your business, customers and sales process.
- Provide a written plan with scope, timeline and a fixed quote.
- Show you real work in short review rounds rather than lengthy presentations.
- Build on a platform your team can edit, and set everything up in your company's name.
- Hand over documentation, logins and training at launch.
- Offer ongoing support so the website keeps improving.

## A one-hour leadership review

If you want to act this month, block one hour with your commercial and marketing leads and work through this agenda:

1. **Ten minutes:** each person looks at the website on their phone as if they were a new customer.
2. **Twenty minutes:** answer the five questions above honestly and note the gaps.
3. **Fifteen minutes:** review the numbers - visits, enquiries, conversion - or agree to start measuring them.
4. **Fifteen minutes:** decide whether you need a refresh, a rebuild or simply better ownership, and who will lead it.

That hour is often the most valuable marketing meeting a leadership team holds all year.

## The bottom line

Your website works for you around the clock, in front of every audience that matters. When it is clear, current, fast and measured, it quietly wins business and trust. When it is neglected, it costs opportunities you never see.

Treat it like the board-level asset it is: give it an owner, hold it to clear measures and invest in it as deliberately as you would in any other part of how your company competes.
