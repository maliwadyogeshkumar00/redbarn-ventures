When an agency project disappoints, the problem is often blamed on creativity, speed or communication. In many cases, the real issue started on day one: a brief that was vague, contradictory or missing the information that mattered most.

A good brief is not long. It is clear. It tells the agency what success looks like, who the work is for and what constraints exist. With that clarity, good agencies can do their best work.

## Why briefs go wrong

The most common problems are easy to recognise:

- **No clear goal.** "We need a new website" describes an output, not a result.
- **Too many decision-makers.** Different stakeholders want different things, and nobody has the final say.
- **Hidden constraints.** Budget, deadlines, technical limits or legal requirements surface halfway through.
- **Solutions instead of problems.** The brief prescribes the answer before the agency understands the question.
- **Missing context.** The agency is not told about customers, competitors or what has been tried before.

Each of these leads to rework, delays and frustration.

> A brief is not a request for work. It is a description of a problem worth solving.

## A simple brief structure

A strong brief answers eight questions:

1. **Background.** What is happening in the business that makes this project necessary now?
2. **Objective.** What should change as a result? Describe the outcome in measurable terms where possible.
3. **Audience.** Who must this work persuade, and what do they currently think or do?
4. **Message.** What is the single most important thing the audience should understand?
5. **Deliverables.** What do you expect to receive?
6. **Constraints.** Budget range, timeline, brand rules, technical or legal limits.
7. **Decision-making.** Who approves the work, and how will feedback be gathered?
8. **Success.** How will you judge whether the project worked?

If you cannot answer one of these, that is useful information. Tell the agency; a good partner will help you work it out.

## Share the uncomfortable truths

The most useful parts of a brief are often the ones companies hesitate to share:

- What did not work last time, and why.
- Internal disagreements about direction.
- Budget realities.
- Concerns about the brand, product or market.

Agencies are not judges. Honest context helps them avoid repeating mistakes and design solutions that work within reality.

## Agree on how decisions will be made

Many projects stall because feedback arrives from too many people at once. Before work begins:

- Name one person responsible for consolidating feedback.
- Agree which stakeholders review at which stage.
- Set clear review dates.
- Decide what happens if opinions conflict.

This simple governance can save weeks.

## Judge work against the brief, not taste

When the work arrives, the first question should not be "Do I like it?" but "Does it achieve what the brief asked for?" Personal preference matters, but it should come after the objective, audience and message have been checked. Teams that review against the brief make faster, better decisions.

## Expect questions - and welcome them

A good agency will challenge parts of your brief. That is a sign of engagement, not difficulty. Questions about your audience, goals or assumptions often lead to stronger work than following the brief to the letter.

## The bottom line

The quality of agency work rarely exceeds the quality of the brief behind it. Spend an extra hour making the problem, audience and success criteria clear, and you will save many hours - and much frustration - later.
