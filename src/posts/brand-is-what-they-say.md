Every company has a brand, whether it designed one or not. It is not the logo on the door or the colour of the website. It is the sentence a customer uses when a colleague asks, "Who are they, and are they any good?" That sentence is formed in seconds and repeated for years. For most businesses, it was never deliberately chosen.

This is the most useful definition of brand for a leadership team, because it moves the conversation away from taste and towards behaviour. You cannot control the sentence directly. You can, however, decide what it should be and then make every touchpoint push towards it.

## Start with the sentence you want

Before thinking about design, write down the sentence you would like a satisfied customer to say about you to a peer. Keep it short, specific and believable. "They are the team that actually understands our industry and never misses a deadline" is a brand. "Innovative solutions for a changing world" is not; nobody has ever said it out loud about a supplier they liked.

Good brand sentences usually contain three things:

- **Who you are for.** "The people you call when the project is complicated."
- **What you are known for.** "Calm, precise and fast to answer."
- **Why it matters.** "You never have to chase them."

If the leadership team cannot agree on this sentence, no amount of design will fix it. Disagreement at the top shows up as inconsistency everywhere else.

## Find the gap between the sentence and reality

Once you know the sentence you want, find out the one people actually use. The fastest way is also the most uncomfortable: ask. Call ten customers, two lost prospects and a few partners. Ask them how they would describe you to someone who has never heard of you, and what nearly stopped them from working with you.

You will usually hear three kinds of answers:

1. **Things you want to be known for** - keep and amplify them.
2. **Things you did not realise mattered** - often the real reason people choose you.
3. **Things that hurt** - slow replies, confusing pricing, an outdated website, a sales process that feels heavy.

The gap between the sentence you want and the one you hear is your brand strategy. Everything else is execution.

## Every touchpoint is a vote

People form their sentence from accumulated evidence. Each interaction is a small vote for or against the description you want:

- The first five seconds on your website.
- How quickly someone replies to an enquiry.
- Whether your proposal looks like it came from a serious company.
- How your team speaks on calls and on stage.
- How problems are handled when something goes wrong.

Most companies invest heavily in a few visible votes, such as a new logo, while ignoring the hundreds of small ones that happen every week. A beautiful identity cannot outvote a slow, confusing customer experience.

> A brand is not built in a launch. It is built in the small moments nobody puts in a press release.

## Make the sentence operational

The difference between companies with strong brands and those without is rarely creativity. It is consistency. The sentence has to become a working tool, not a slide in a strategy deck.

Practical ways to do that:

- **Write it on the brief for everything.** Every website page, campaign and sales document should be checked against it.
- **Turn it into standards.** If "fast to answer" is part of the sentence, define what fast means and measure it.
- **Hire and train for it.** People who naturally behave the way the sentence describes will carry it further than any guideline.
- **Design to support it.** Visual identity, tone of voice and layout should make the sentence feel true at a glance.

## Design is the amplifier, not the source

Good design matters enormously, but it amplifies a brand rather than creating one. A clear, confident identity makes a strong reputation easier to recognise and remember. It cannot invent a reputation the company has not earned.

That is why the best brand projects start with the sentence and the evidence, and only then move to logos, colour and type. Designed this way, every visual choice has a reason, and the identity feels inevitable rather than decorative.

## Measure what people say

You cannot measure a brand with a single number, but you can track whether the sentence is spreading. Useful signals include:

- The words customers use in reviews, referrals and sales calls.
- How prospects describe you when they first get in touch.
- How often you are invited into conversations without competing on price.
- Whether new hires can describe the company the way you would.

Review these every quarter. When the words people use start matching the sentence you wrote, the brand is working.

## The leadership question

A brand is too important to delegate entirely to marketing, and too practical to leave to taste. It is a leadership decision about what the company wants to be known for, followed by the discipline to make every interaction prove it.

Decide the sentence. Close the gap. Then let design make it impossible to miss.
